A mixed fleet: tessera and the engine in one committee

Every volume so far has one kind of participant: a ROS 2 node talking to vertex_node. Real fleets are rarely so tidy. This volume puts two Pioneer sweepers and two DJI drones in a single Vertex committee, where the robots join through tessera and the drones link the consensus engine straight into a Rust binary with no ROS in the process at all. They share exactly two things, and everything else about them differs.

The series. 1 · Concepts and protocol design  →  2 · The fleet API  →  3 · Crate-level internals  →  4 · Porting  →  5 · Mixed fleets (you are here). Volume 3 is the prerequisite that matters: this volume uses the engine directly and assumes you have read its concurrency contract.

You need: the tessera workspace built, Docker for the headless run, and optionally Webots R2025a for the live world. The worked example is vertex_ros2/test/simulation_airground/.

Where this fits

Volume 3 showed how to embed vertex_core in a process that cannot be a ROS 2 node. It stopped at a single process. The obvious next question is the one this volume answers: if one member of your fleet is not a ROS node, can it still be a full peer of the ones that are, and how would you know?

The answer has to be demonstrated rather than asserted, because the failure mode is silent. Two implementations of the same protocol can agree for an hour and then diverge on one unusual record, and nothing in the transport will tell you. So the example is built to make agreement checkable, and most of this volume is about that checkability rather than about drones.

What we are building

An arena, fifty metres square, with trees, rocks, a herd of deer and one crater. Twelve ground sectors to sweep, grouped into six survey blocks. Four agents:

Ground tier — two Pioneer 3-AT sweepers

Ordinary tessera citizens, exactly as Volumes 1 and 2 describe.

  • one vertex_node process each
  • a VertexAgent subclass folding /vertex/event
  • a horizontal Sick LMS 291 lidar, which cannot see a hole

Air tier — two Mavic 2 PRO surveyors

Native Rust binaries. No rclrs, no DDS, no message packages, no rosbridge.

  • tashi-vertex linked directly
  • their own fold, in Rust
  • a downward laser ranger, which can

The asymmetry in those last two bullets is the whole scenario. A crater is invisible to a lidar mounted horizontally at 0.32 m and unmistakable to a ranger looking straight down from twelve metres. So the ground tier depends on the air tier for information it cannot gather, and the ordered log is what turns that dependency into a map both robots agree on.

Two properties fall out, and they are worth separating because only the first is about heterogeneity:

  • Ground work is gated on air work. A sector is unclaimable until some drone has reported it surveyed. The sweepers visibly sit still at the start of a run, which is correct behaviour and not a stall. They are not waiting for a message; they are waiting for a position in an agreed history.
  • Hazards need corroboration. One witness defers a sector. Two distinct witnesses condemn it. Consensus makes every peer agree on what was said. It does not make what was said true, and the gap between those two is where a protocol either has a rule or has a bug.

Two ways into one committee

Vertex does not know or care what language its peers are written in, or whether they have a ROS graph attached. A peer is a public key, a UDP address, and a process that signs events. Tessera is one way to be that process; linking the engine is another.

Through tesseraEngine directly
What runsvertex_node plus your agentyour binary, linking tashi-vertex
Submit a recordpublish on /vertex/txengine.send_transaction(tx)
Receive the ordersubscribe /vertex/eventengine.recv_message().await
Lifecycle/vertex/transition serviceEngine::start and process exit

Choose the second only when the first is genuinely unavailable: a flight controller you cannot put a ROS graph on, a vendor SDK that owns the main loop, a device too small for DDS. The engine path is not faster or purer. It is what you use when a ROS node is not an option, and it hands you three sharp edges that vertex_core would otherwise have handled. All three are in this volume.

Step 1Pin the engine version

This is the one thing a mixed fleet must get right, and it is the easiest to get wrong. Every peer has to link the same engine protocol version. A drone built against a different tashi-vertex than vertex_node will not form a committee with it, and the symptom is peers that never finalize rather than a clear error.

So both sides read their version from the same place. vertex_core pins it exactly:

toml — vertex_core/Cargo.toml
[dependencies]
tashi-vertex = "=0.14.0"

and the drone binary repeats that exact requirement:

toml — air_agent/Cargo.toml
[dependencies]
# The SAME pinned version vertex_core uses. Both tiers must link one engine
# protocol version or they cannot form a committee. Deliberately `=`, not `^`.
tashi-vertex = "=0.14.0"
tokio = { version = "1", features = ["rt", "macros", "net", "time", "io-util", "sync"] }
serde_json = "1"
Why = and not ^. A caret requirement lets a routine cargo update move the engine underneath a release. With an exact requirement the version lives in the tree, so checking out a tag reproduces the build, and the engine version is not something recorded out of band and hoped for. The tashi-vertex crate in turn pins the tashi-vertex-c release its build script downloads, so one tag identifies the whole chain.
The FFI types are not interchangeable across versions. If cargo resolves two different tashi-vertex versions in one dependency graph, you get two incompatible copies of KeySecret, Engine and friends, and the mismatch surfaces as a type error that says nothing about versions. Match the pin exactly.

Step 2One committee, mixed members

The committee is four peers, so it tolerates one Byzantine or crashed member under n ≥ 3f + 1. Nothing in the key material says which tier a peer belongs to, and that is the point: the engine cannot tell them apart.

json — fixtures/peers_airground.json
[
  {"name": "bot_0",   "tier": "ground", "secret": "<base58>", "public": "<base58>", "addr": "127.0.0.1:47631"},
  {"name": "bot_1",   "tier": "ground", "secret": "<base58>", "public": "<base58>", "addr": "127.0.0.1:47632"},
  {"name": "drone_0", "tier": "air",    "secret": "<base58>", "public": "<base58>", "addr": "127.0.0.1:47633"},
  {"name": "drone_1", "tier": "air",    "secret": "<base58>", "public": "<base58>", "addr": "127.0.0.1:47634"}
]

The tier field is for the launch file's benefit, deciding what to start for each peer. The engine never sees it. Generate the file with fixtures/gen_peers_airground.sh, which uses vertex_core's key-generate example so nothing outside the checkout is needed.

The launch file then starts two quite different things from one list:

python — airground.launch.py, condensed
for me in BOTS:                       # ground tier: tessera
    ns = f"/{me}"
    actions.append(Node(
        package="vertex_ros2", executable="vertex_node",
        remappings=[(f"/vertex/{t}", f"{ns}/vertex/{t}") for t in CONTRACT_TOPICS],
        parameters=[{
            "vertex.bind_address": peers[me]["addr"],
            "vertex.secret_key_path": key_file(peers[me]["secret"]),
            "vertex.peers": peer_specs(peers, me),   # "<public>@<addr>", the others
            "options.heartbeat_us": 50000,
        }]))
    actions.append(Node(executable=COORDINATOR, namespace=ns,
                        parameters=[{"agent_id": me, "agents": AGENTS, **GRID}]))

for me in DRONES:                     # air tier: no ROS at all
    cmd = [AIR_AGENT, "--id", me,
           "--bind", peers[me]["addr"],
           "--key", peers[me]["secret"],
           "--link", f"0.0.0.0:{LINK_PORT[me]}"]
    for spec in peer_specs(peers, me):
        cmd += ["--peer", spec]
    actions.append(ExecuteProcess(cmd=cmd))
Note ExecuteProcess, not Node. The drone agents are not ROS nodes and launch treats them as plain processes. They appear in the same launch description purely so one command brings up the whole fleet; there is no ROS relationship between them and anything else.

Step 3Join from plain Rust

Five calls put you in the committee. Peers collects who is out there, including yourself; Context owns the async runtime the C library uses; Socket binds your UDP address; Options carries the tunables; Engine::start begins participating.

rust — air_agent/src/main.rs, the bring-up
use tashi_vertex::{Context, Engine, KeyPublic, KeySecret, Message, Options, Peers,
                   Socket, Transaction};

// Everyone else, then yourself. The engine derives the session size from the
// set, and n >= 3f + 1 applies to the total including you.
let mut peers = Peers::with_capacity(args.peers.len() + 1)?;
for (addr, public) in &args.peers {
    peers.insert(addr, public, Default::default())?;
}
peers.insert(&args.bind, &args.key.public(), Default::default())?;

let context = Context::new()?;
let socket = Socket::bind(&context, &args.bind).await?;

let mut options = Options::default();
// Match what the launch file gives vertex_node, so every peer in the mesh
// gossips at the same cadence.
options.set_heartbeat_us(50_000);

let engine = Rc::new(Engine::start(&context, socket, options, &args.key, peers, false)?);

That final false is joining_running_session. It stays false here because all four peers start together. Set it true only when a peer joins a session that is already producing events, and read the safe-usage envelope in vertex_ros2/README.md before you do.

Submitting a record is a plain call, not a future:

rust — one transaction
fn submit(engine: &Engine, rec: &serde_json::Value) {
    // serde_json's default Map is a BTreeMap, so this is sorted-key compact
    // JSON: byte-identical to vertex_fleet.state.encode on the Python side.
    let bytes = rec.to_string().into_bytes();
    let mut tx = Transaction::allocate(bytes.len());
    tx.copy_from_slice(&bytes);
    if let Err(e) = engine.send_transaction(tx) {
        eprintln!("send_transaction failed: {e:?}");
    }
}
Canonical bytes matter more than you would think. Both tiers must serialize a given record to the same bytes, or the two folds will disagree about duplicate suppression even when their rules are identical. Python's encode uses sorted keys and compact separators; serde_json's default map is a BTreeMap, which produces exactly that. Enable its preserve_order feature and you break the property silently.

Three traps in the engine API

Volume 3 documents the concurrency contract. Here is what it costs in practice, because all three of these cost me a debugging session.

1. recv_message() is not cancellation-safe

Its future registers a pointer to itself with the C library on first poll. Drop it mid-flight, which is exactly what tokio::select! does to a losing branch, and a later callback writes into freed memory. It does not fail at the call site. It surfaces as heap corruption far away:

what it actually looks like
malloc(): unsorted double linked list corrupted
[air_agent] process has died, exit code -6

The fix is the shape vertex_core already uses. The handles are !Send, so a current-thread runtime and a LocalSet with two cooperative tasks over an Rc<Engine>: a receive loop that is never cancelled, and a control loop that selects only over cancellation-safe futures.

rust — the two-task shape
// Task 1: never cancelled. The ONLY writer to the folded state, which is the
// same single-mutation-path rule VertexAgent gives you for free.
async fn recv_loop(engine: Rc<Engine>, state: Rc<RefCell<AirGroundState>>) {
    loop {
        match engine.recv_message().await {
            Ok(Some(Message::Event(ev))) => {
                let mut st = state.borrow_mut();
                for payload in ev.transactions() {
                    if let Ok(rec) = serde_json::from_slice(payload) { st.apply(&rec); }
                }
            }
            Ok(Some(Message::SyncPoint(_))) => {}
            Ok(None) | Err(_) => return,
        }
    }
}

// Task 2: every future here is cancellation-safe (a timer, an accept, a
// buffered line read). send_transaction is a plain call, so proposing from
// here needs no channel back to task 1.
let local = tokio::task::LocalSet::new();
local.spawn_local(recv_loop(Rc::clone(&engine), Rc::clone(&state)));
local.run_until(control_loop(engine, state, listener)).await;

2. Nothing gives you an rpath

The tashi-vertex build script adds an rpath to its own test and example binaries, not to yours. Your binary links libtashi-vertex and then cannot find it at load time. cargo test and cargo run hide this by injecting a library path, so it appears only when something else executes the binary, which for us was the launch file:

the symptom
dyld[49198]: Library not loaded: @rpath/libtashi-vertex.dylib
  Reason: no LC_RPATH's found

Copy vertex_core/build.rs into your crate. It locates the library under the shared target directory and bakes an absolute rpath plus $ORIGIN-relative fallbacks. On Linux you can instead export LD_LIBRARY_PATH the way docker/entrypoint.sh does in export_tv_libpath.

3. You own the single-mutation-path rule now

VertexAgent enforces it structurally: shared state changes in the event callback and nowhere else. Off the ROS path nothing stops you mutating the fold from a timer, and if you do, your peer will drift from the fleet in a way no test of your own will catch. Keeping the receive loop the only writer, as above, is what replaces the enforcement.

Step 4The shared protocol

Both tiers fold the same records. Agent ids are strings rather than the integers the earlier simulations used, because in a mixed fleet the id has to say which tier a peer is on.

jsonc — the record set
{ "op": "survey_claim", "agent": "drone_0", "block": "B03", "epoch": 0 }
{ "op": "surveyed",     "agent": "drone_0", "block": "B03",
                        "cells": ["S06", "S07"], "epoch": 0 }
{ "op": "hazard",       "agent": "drone_0", "cell": "S05", "kind": "pit", "epoch": 0 }
{ "op": "corroborate",  "agent": "bot_1",   "cell": "S05", "epoch": 0 }
{ "op": "claim",        "agent": "bot_0",   "sector": "S06", "epoch": 0 }
{ "op": "explored",     "agent": "bot_0",   "sector": "S06", "epoch": 0 }
{ "op": "abandon",      "agent": "bot_0",   "sector": "S06", "epoch": 0 }
{ "op": "rtb",          "agent": "drone_0", "epoch": 0 }
{ "op": "ready",        "agent": "drone_0", "epoch": 0 }
{ "op": "health",       "agent": "drone_1", "seq": 41, "ok": true, "epoch": 0 }
{ "op": "suspect",      "agent": "bot_0",   "victim": "drone_1", "seen_seq": 41, "epoch": 0 }
{ "op": "reset",        "epoch": 1 }

Six rules turn those into shared state. The first two are ordinary; the third is the one worth stealing.

RuleWhat it buys
Air blocks and ground sectors are two claim namespaces under one exclusivity rule. First claim in consensus order wins; an agent holds at most one thing.The same primitive serves two vehicle classes with no special-casing.
A sector is unclaimable until a drone reports it surveyed.A real dependency between tiers, ordered by the log rather than by wall clock.
A hazard is provisional. A second distinct agent promotes it to confirmed, which condemns the sector.Separates agreement from truth. A peer with valid signatures can say anything; this decides what the fleet acts on.
rtb releases a drone's block and grounds it until ready.A lease, motivated by battery instead of by silence.
health beacons and the suspect silence lease apply uniformly across tiers.A bot may suspect a drone and a drone may suspect a bot. The fold does not distinguish them.
Done when every block is surveyed and every sector is explored or condemned.A termination condition spanning both tiers.

Note that hazard and corroborate fold identically. What matters is how many distinct agents have vouched for a cell, not which verb carried the claim. That is why a bot stuck in a crater and a drone seeing one from above are interchangeable as evidence, and why the rule needs no notion of which tier is more trustworthy.

Step 5Keep the folds honest

The fold now exists more than once: Python for the robots, Rust for the drones, and a third copy in JavaScript so the viewer can decode the event stream itself rather than trust a summary. Three copies of a rule is precisely how a protocol drifts, and the drift is silent.

So all three are pinned to one file. fixtures/conformance.json holds a scripted record log and the canonical state snapshot after every record:

json — fixtures/conformance.json, shape
{
  "geometry":    { "nx": 4, "ny": 3, "cell_w": 10.0, "block_w": 2, ... },
  "sectors":     ["S00", "S01", ...],
  "blocks":      ["B00", ...],
  "block_cells": { "B00": ["S00", "S01"], ... },
  "log":         [ {"op": "health", "agent": "bot_0", "seq": 0, "ok": true, "epoch": 0}, ... ],
  "snapshots":   [ { "claimed": {}, "surveyed": [], ... }, ... ]
}

Each implementation replays it and compares after each record, so a divergence fails at the record that caused it instead of at the end of a run:

shell — one fixture, three languages
python3 nodes/test_airground_fsm.py        # the robots' fold
(cd air_agent && cargo test)               # the drones' fold
node viewer/airground_fold.test.js         # the viewer's fold

Regenerate with python3 fixtures/gen_conformance.py after any intentional change, and read the diff carefully: a change there is a change to the protocol every tier implements.

Write the log to include records that should do nothing. Half the value is in the rejections: a claim before its survey landed, a survey report from a peer that does not hold the block, a stale health beacon, a suspicion voided by a late beacon, a duplicate hazard from one agent. An implementation that accepts too much passes a happy-path fixture easily.
Keep the snapshot minimal and canonical. Sort every collection, and keep anything view-specific out of it. Our viewer tracks per-sector provenance for its inspector, and that deliberately sits outside the snapshot so it cannot force the other two implementations to change.

Step 6Run it

Headless, no Webots, everything asserted:

shell
docker compose run --rm sim simtest        # all three simulations

The air/ground portion runs both folds' unit tests, then three launch tests: the mixed fleet, a drone that invents hazards, and a drone that hides one. There is also an air-tier-only smoke test that needs neither ROS nor Docker, four real agents in a real committee over UDP, which is the fastest way to debug the Rust half:

shell
(cd air_agent && cargo build)
bash fixtures/gen_peers_airground.sh
python3 nodes/smoke_air_committee.py

For the live world, the consensus graph runs in the container and Webots runs natively:

shell — terminal 1, then terminal 2
docker compose run --rm --service-ports sim simairground

open -a Webots vertex_ros2/test/simulation_airground/worlds/airground_arena.wbt

What you should see, in order: the drones climb to twelve metres and begin lawnmower passes while the Pioneers sit still; sectors become claimable behind them and the sweeping starts; the crater is flagged with one witness and deliberately left for last; a Pioneer eventually drives into it, declines to credit the sector because it is below grade, reports being stuck, and that second witness condemns it. Expect a robot to finish the run sitting in the hole. That is the design working, not a failure.

Two panels are worth knowing about. Click any sector and the inspector narrates its history: which drone surveyed it, which robots raced for the claim and which lost, how many times each gave up, who witnessed a hazard and in what order, who finally reached the centre. A claim marked “lost the race” before any survey landed is the gate rejecting it, which is the scenario in miniature.

The consensus panel puts all four agents' views side by side, and a cell is highlighted only when a disagreement persists for more than a second, so ordinary publish-timing skew is not mistaken for divergence. The drone columns are labelled (fold) because the drone agents publish nothing to ROS at all; that column is the page's own fold of the event stream. Their airframes do publish a pose, for observers only, which is why the diamonds move. Nothing reads that back and no decision depends on it.

Pitfalls I hit so you don't have to

  • A version mismatch does not announce itself. Peers simply never finalize. Check the pin on both sides first, before you debug anything else.
  • Do not put recv_message() in a select!. The corruption appears somewhere unrelated, minutes later.
  • Sample sensors continuously, not at waypoints. Our drones sampled the ranger once per waypoint arrival, which has a tolerance, so the single reading landed off-centre where a crater is shallow. They flew over real craters and reported clean ground.
  • Reaching a sector is not the same as covering it. A robot that has fallen into a crater can be within the coverage radius of the centre and still have swept nothing. Ours credited the sector from inside the hole until its pose carried a real altitude and the coordinator learned to refuse while below grade.
  • Not every obstacle is the one you modelled. A tree cluster stops a Pioneer just as well as a crater and no drone sees it from above. Without a path to condemnation, a robot claims, stalls, abandons and re-claims forever. Repeated first-hand failure has to count as evidence too.
  • A warm build directory hides real breakage. Ours concealed a workspace that could not build from a clean checkout at all, for a week, while every local run passed. Wipe the volumes occasionally and find out what your CI already knows.
